    Initial prototype write-up

    What have we done so far?

    We have built an interactive prototype comparing local air quality with separate temperature projections. A D3 bar chart shows IQAir’s reported 2023 annual PM2.5 averages for Shanghai, Los Angeles, and Reykjavík, using the same year and units for all three cities. Selecting a city highlights its bar and updates its reported value while keeping all three cities visible. The D3 line chart keeps the original global series and adds 774 World Bank climate-grid records near the three cities, covering 2015 through 2100 under the same three scenarios. Users can hover or tap the temperature chart, or use the keyboard-accessible year slider, to compare yearly values while the scenario scale stays fixed for the selected location. The temperature location selector updates the lines, location label, tooltips, and yearly values for Global Average, Shanghai, Los Angeles, or Reykjavík. We have also added readable selection states and messages for empty selections or unavailable data.

    What will be most challenging to design, and why?

    The most challenging design problem is comparing city-level pollution and global climate outcomes without suggesting that they measure the same thing. The bar chart uses annual PM2.5 concentrations, while the line chart uses temperature values across different future scenarios. We need concise explanations that preserve this distinction and make the two interactions easy to understand. Monitoring coverage differs across cities, and a citywide annual average can hide daily peaks and neighborhood differences. The city temperature series represent nearby 0.25-degree climate-grid cells rather than neighborhood forecasts, and the original global dataset still needs its source verified. Our next step is to refine the guided explanation using feedback on the comparisons and the amount of context viewers need.

    Two different environmental questions

    In this three-city sample, Shanghai’s reported 2023 annual PM2.5 concentration is highest and Reykjavík’s is lowest. That comparison describes local particle pollution in one year; it does not rank the cities’ greenhouse-gas emissions or tell us how quickly they will warm. Reading it alongside global scenarios helps separate the air people breathe locally from longer-term global climate outcomes.
