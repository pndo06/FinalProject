    Initial prototype write-up

    What have we done so far?

    We have built an interactive prototype comparing local air quality with separate global temperature trajectories. A D3 bar chart shows IQAir’s reported 2023 annual PM2.5 averages for Shanghai, Los Angeles, and Reykjavík, with units, source links, and monitoring caveats. Selecting a city highlights its bar and updates its reported value while keeping all three cities visible. The existing D3 line chart uses 258 global scenario records from 2015 through 2100 and retains scenario toggles and year inspection. Users can hover or tap the global chart, or use the keyboard-accessible year slider, to compare yearly values on a fixed vertical scale. We have also added readable selection states and messages for empty selections or unavailable data.

    What will be most challenging to design, and why?

    The most challenging design problem is comparing city-level pollution and global climate outcomes without suggesting that they measure the same thing. The city chart uses annual PM2.5 concentrations, while the global chart uses temperature values across different future scenarios. We need concise explanations that preserve this distinction and make the two interactions easy to understand. Monitoring coverage differs across cities, and a citywide annual average can hide daily peaks and neighborhood differences. We still need to verify the original global dataset’s provider, model, processing method, and temperature baseline before making stronger climate claims. Our next step is to refine the guided explanation using feedback on the comparisons and the amount of context viewers need.

    Two different environmental questions

    In this three-city sample, Shanghai’s reported 2023 annual PM2.5 concentration is highest and Reykjavík’s is lowest. That comparison describes local particle pollution in one year; it does not rank the cities’ greenhouse-gas emissions or tell us how quickly they will warm. Reading it alongside global scenarios helps separate the air people breathe locally from longer-term global climate outcomes.
